### 2701 · R0205

**Is Artificial Intelligence Making Medical Students Smarter—or More Dependent?**

2026 · Indian Journal of Community Medicine · article · en · https://doi.org/10.4103/ijcm.ijcm_324_25

Sir/Madam, The integration of artificial intelligence (AI) into medical education is accelerating rapidly, spurred by the widespread availability of AI-powered tools such as ChatGPT, Google Bard, and various clinical decision support systems. Medical students increasingly use these technologies for studying, drafting assignments, preparing for exams, and even generating case-based discussions. While AI offers considerable benefits in enhancing access to knowledge and streamlining learning, it also raises a vital pedagogical and ethical question: Is AI making medical students smarter—or simply more dependent? The potential for AI to revolutionize medical education is undeniable. With its ability to process vast amounts of data and deliver personalized responses, AI tools are now being used for everything from instant explanations of complex topics to practice quizzes and clinical simulations. Actually, studies have shown that AI-powered applications can significantly improve academic performance when used alongside traditional study methods.[1] Medical students benefit from on-demand access to information, bypassing the need to search through textbooks or lecture notes. AI chatbots, for example, can explain difficult concepts in simple terms, often in an interactive, conversational style. This has made AI an effective personal tutor, allowing for tailored explanations based on the student’s existing knowledge level and learning speed.[2] Additionally, AI tools support self-directed learning. In the competency-based medical education (CBME) model, where learners progress at their own pace, AI platforms can track progress and suggest next steps, ensuring continuous and targeted improvement.[3] Tools like virtual patient simulators and differential diagnosis generators also allow students to engage in clinical reasoning exercises without needing access to a clinical setting. However, the other side of the coin reveals several concerns. As AI tools become more integrated into the daily learning routines of students, a pattern of over-reliance is emerging. Many students now turn to AI as their primary source of information, even for tasks that traditionally demanded critical thinking and literature review. There is growing evidence that excessive dependence on AI may hinder the development of cognitive and analytical skills that are essential for clinical practice. When students outsource problem-solving to AI tools without engaging with the reasoning process, they may miss opportunities to cultivate deep understanding.[4] Moreover, academic integrity has come under threat. AI-generated content is increasingly used to draft essays, answer examination questions, and complete assignments. While AI can assist in writing and proofreading, its misuse undermines genuine learning and originality. A recent study highlighted that more than 40% of students admitted to using AI-generated content in their submissions without attribution.[5] Another major concern is the erosion of foundational knowledge. In clinical settings where time is limited and high-stakes decisions are made, relying on AI-generated suggestions may not be feasible or safe. A solid grounding in physiology, pathology, and pharmacology is essential for intuitive, evidence-based decision-making. If students habitually defer to AI, their retention of this core knowledge may be jeopardized.[6] The challenge is not whether AI should be used in medical education, but how it should be used responsibly and ethically. Instead of banning AI tools, institutions should adopt a proactive approach: integrating AI literacy and ethics into the medical curriculum. Students must be trained in not only how to use AI tools but also how to critically evaluate and cross-verify the information they provide. Educators must also evolve. Teaching strategies should include assignments that require synthesis, critical appraisal, and real-world application—skills that cannot be outsourced to an algorithm. Rather than penalizing AI usage, educators can design assessments that incorporate it meaningfully, for example, by asking students to critique an AI-generated response using peer-reviewed literature. Furthermore, academic policies must be updated to address the ethical dimensions of AI use. Universities should develop clear guidelines around what constitutes acceptable and unacceptable AI-assisted work, much like existing policies on plagiarism and citation standards.[7] The way forward involves striking a delicate balance. When used judiciously, AI can help students become more knowledgeable, self-reliant, and prepared for modern medical practice. It offers a unique opportunity to complement human learning, not replace it. For example, AI can help reduce the cognitive load by simplifying initial exposure to new concepts, allowing students to invest more effort in advanced comprehension and clinical application. However, unchecked dependency on AI can lead to a generation of practitioners who may be excellent at retrieving information but poor at interpreting or applying it in nuanced, patient-centered ways. Medicine is, after all, as much an art as it is a science. Empathy, clinical intuition, and ethical judgment cannot be programmed—they must be nurtured through rigorous, reflective education. To conclude, AI is a double-edged sword in the hands of medical students. It has the power to make them smarter, more efficient, and better prepared for a technology-driven healthcare future. But it also risks making them dependent, passive consumers of information. The solution lies in guided integration, ethical oversight, and a renewed focus on critical thinking and clinical reasoning. Let us harness the power of AI—not to replace the human brain, but to empower it. Authors contribution This letter to editor was done by Dr. Mary Chandrika Anton. Purpose: Artificial intelligence (AI) is increasingly promoted as a transformative tool for improving mathematics learning, yet its educational value remains contested when issues of access, teacher readiness, and pedagogical safeguards are considered. This study aims to critically examine whether AI genuinely improves mathematics learning in secondary education by synthesizing evidence on its opportunities, risks, and safeguards. Method: This study employed a Systematic Literature Review (SLR) following the PRISMA 2020 guidelines. A total of 40 articles were selected from an initial pool of 309 records indexed in ERIC, Web of Science, and Scopus between 2021 and 2025. The selected studies were analyzed using thematic synthesis through open, axial, and selective coding to identify recurring patterns related to AI-supported mathematics learning, implementation risks, and responsible pedagogical practices. Findings: The findings indicate that AI can support mathematics learning through adaptive personalization, intelligent tutoring systems, automated assessment, real-time feedback, and increased student motivation. However, the review also identifies substantial risks, including unequal digital access, insufficient teacher readiness, weak TPACK-based integration, superficial learning, and dependence on generative AI. One reviewed study reported that students using generative AI without adequate guardrails performed 17% worse after AI access was removed, highlighting the need for responsible implementation. Significance: This review concludes that AI does not automatically improve mathematics learning. Its effectiveness depends on pedagogical safeguards, teacher mediation, equitable infrastructure, and blended instructional design. The study contributes a critical evidence-based framework for integrating AI responsibly in secondary mathematics education and provides practical guidance for teachers, school leaders, policymakers, and educational technology developers.

The healthcare system in Iraq is currently undergoing development and AI has not yet been adopted in clinical practice in Erbil; in addition, no formal AI instruction has been incorporated into the medical education curriculum. OBJECTIVE The aim of this study was to assess knowledge levels, attitudes, and perceptions regarding AI among medical students and healthcare professionals in Erbil, Kurdistan Region of Iraq. METHODS This survey was distributed to medical students and residents in Erbil, Kurdistan Region of Iraq. The survey was adapted from Teng et al, and modified to reflect the local context. Chi-square and Fisher's exact tests were used to test associations between categorical variables. The framework offers a coherent yet adaptable model for institutions seeking to maintain valid and defensible assessment practices in the age of generative AI.

2024 · openRxiv · posted-content · https://doi.org/10.1101/2024.01.08.24300995

Abstract The advances in large language models (LLMs) are evolving rapidly. Artificial intelligence (AI) chatbots based on LLMs excel in language understanding and generation, with potential utility to transform healthcare education and practice. However, it is important to assess the performance of such AI models in various topics to highlight its strengths and possible limitations. Therefore, this study aimed to evaluate the performance of ChatGPT (GPT-3.5 and GPT-4), Bing, and Bard compared to human students at a postgraduate master’s (MSc) level in Medical Laboratory Sciences. The study design was based on the METRICS checklist for the design and reporting of AI-based studies in healthcare. The study utilized a dataset of 60 Clinical Chemistry multiple-choice questions (MCQs) initially conceived for assessment of 20 MSc students. The revised Bloom’s taxonomy was used as the framework for classifying the MCQs into four cognitive categories: Remember, Understand, Analyze, and Apply. A modified version of the CLEAR tool was used for assessment of the quality of AI-generated content, with Cohen’s κ for inter-rater agreement. The findings indicated that ChatGPT-4 excelled in the Clinical Chemistry exam, while ChatGPT-3.5, Bing, and Bard were above-average. Given that the MCQs were directed to postgraduate students with a high degree of specialization, the performance of these AI chatbots was remarkable. Due to the risks of academic dishonesty and possible dependence on these AI models, the appropriateness of MCQs as an assessment tool in higher education should be re-evaluated.
