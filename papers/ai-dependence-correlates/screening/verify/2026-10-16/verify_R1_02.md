**Mindful technology use, escapist AI chatbot use, and context-specific AI chatbot dependency in adolescents**

2026 · OSF Registries · studyregistration/pre-registration, preprint · https://doi.org/10.17605/osf.io/azd7u

This registration tests whether two process propositions from the transdiagnostic cognitive-behavioural model of social media use (TCBC; Tibber &amp; Silver, 2022), and the wider mindful-technology-use literature, are relevant to adolescent AI chatbot dependency. The question is whether whether mindful, intentional, values-consistent engagement is associated with lower dependency, and whether escapist or avoidant engagement is associated with higher dependency. The rationale is grounded in two related literatures. First, the TCBC proposes that the psychological consequences of digital engagement depend less on simple exposure and more on how, why, and in what context a person uses a technology (Tibber &amp; Silver, 2022). Second, evidence from social-media and problematic-internet-use research links lower mindfulness to more problematic use and suggests that mindfulness-based or mindful-use approaches may be relevant to prevention or behaviour change (Fendel et al., 2024; Meynadier et al., 2024; Sandra, 2025). Our survey does not directly measure all of those domains, so this registration is deliberately limited to MTU and escapism as two central, measurable process variables. AI chatbots differ from social media in important ways, but extending the two focal propositions is theoretically plausible. Adolescent AI chatbot use is becoming common and varied to include schoolwork, information seeking, advice, entertainment, and more private forms of support (McClain et al., 2026). Chatbots are interactive, private, available on demand, and conversational. They can support instrumental competence-related goals, such as schoolwork, studying, revision, assignments, and work-related tasks, but they can also be used for relatedness- and acceptance-related purposes, such as family advice, relationship advice, friendship problems, emotional support, and other private conversations. These properties make adolescent chatbot use a useful context for testing whether mindful engagement and escapist engagement map onto dependency-like outcomes outside conventional social media. The survey strengthens this test by randomly assigning each participant to answer downstream AI-specific scales with one of two use contexts in mind: school/work or non-school/private. The dependency, MTU, and escapism items are therefore context-anchored by repeated prompt instructions. The item wording is not rewritten separately for each domain; context-specificity comes from the randomised prompt and repeated instruction. We use the term context-anchored to make this explicit. This is a cross-sectional association study. We therefore avoid causal language such as “mindful use prevents dependency” in the registered tests. The primary analyses estimate whether MTU and escapism are statistically associated with dependency within this observed sample, after accounting for randomised prompt condition and, where feasible, school clustering.

**Multilevel statistical predictors of context-specific AI chatbot dependency in adolescents**

This registration concerns the broader multilevel predictors paper from the adolescent AI chatbot dependency study. The central purpose is to identify which measured characteristics are most strongly associated with context-specific AI chatbot dependency, and which candidate variables contribute unique variance once overlapping predictors are considered within and across conceptually related classes. The paper is motivated by two goals. This does not imply temporal precedence, causation, prevention, or validated out-of-sample forecasting. The terms risk indicator, resilience indicator, and protective indicator are used descriptively and conceptually, not causally. The questionnaire structure adds a substantive contextual layer. Participants complete one survey, but the downstream AI-specific scales are answered with one randomly assigned use domain in mind: school/work use or non-school/private use. The randomised condition is therefore treated as a fixed design factor and as a potential source of predictor heterogeneity. Because recruitment is conducted through schools in the UK, the design also has a clustered sampling structure. Pupils within the same school may be more similar than pupils from different schools because of shared socioeconomic intake, school AI policies, device access, homework norms, safeguarding guidance, teacher expectations, and in particular peer norms. This registration therefore treats school membership as a design feature to be described, modelled where feasible, and used to qualify precision and generalizability. It does not treat school-level effects as causal evidence.
